# 41 — Physical models: Hammerwork Grand, Skinwork Kit, Fretwire bodies

Two machines synthesize their sound by simulating the instrument's
mechanics, sample by sample:

- `grand` (**Hammerwork Grand**) is a piano: per key, felt hammers strike
  stiff strings that drive a soundboard through a moving bridge.
- `kit` (**Skinwork Kit**) is a drum kit: sticks and beaters strike
  membranes and cymbals.

Fretwire's bass and guitar (`bass`, `guitar`) build the instrument around
their waveguide strings the same way: pickups, wiring and a body every
string shares (§Fretwire: pickups, wiring and bodies).

They share a small layer of physical-modeling kernels,
`kernels/10-physical/`. **Status:** built 2026-10-16 on branch
`claude/physical-piano-drums`.

**Clean-room.** Everything here comes from the published literature
cited inline and from measurements of CC0 recordings. Nothing comes from
another physical-modeling codebase or its data. The scale curves,
hammer and kit constants are literature values or our own estimates, and
each is marked below.

Code:
- `kernels/10-physical/modal.fy`: exact modal step, T60 → decay rate,
  implicit power-law contact.
- `kernels/10-physical/bessel.fy`: the circular membrane's first 40
  modes, generated by `tools/fit/bessel.py`.
- `kernels/06-voices/piano.fy` and `machines/grand/`: the piano.
- `kernels/06-voices/kit.fy` and `machines/kit/`: the kit.
- `kernels/06-voices/string.fy` (the strings and pickups),
  `kernels/06-voices/string_body.fy` (bridge, body, wiring, open strings)
  and `machines/bass/`, `machines/guitar/`: Fretwire.

## The building blocks (`kernels/10-physical`)

| word | what it is | source |
|---|---|---|
| `zoh-coefs` `modal-step` | one damped mode advanced exactly under a force held for the sample: the undamped closed form plus a decay factor. It is stable at any frequency below Nyquist and keeps the asked-for decay at any pitch. | modal synthesis, Adrien 1991 |
| `t60>sigma` | the decay rate that falls 60 dB in T60 | — |
| `contact-solve` | a striker pressed into a body with `F = K e^p (1 + α ė)`, solved implicitly by four Newton steps on the scalar compliance equation `e = e_free − D F`. It is stable for treble-hammer stiffness, where an explicit force blows up. | Hall 1986, Stulov 1995 (felt); Hunt & Crossley 1975 (damping); Bilbao 2009 ch. 4 |
| `memb-ratio` `memb-m` `memb-shape-at` | circular membrane modes: Bessel-zero ratios j_mn/j_01, nodal diameters, and the radial shape J_m(j_mn r/a) at any radius | Rayleigh; Fletcher & Rossing §3.6 |

A body is a bank of these modes in fixed-size state arrays, unrolled with
`times`. Each mode's coefficients are built at note-on, so the render only
multiplies and adds. The words take physical units (metres, newtons,
kilograms), so a striker's character comes from its mass and felt law,
not from a filter.

Other techniques the two voices use, each a candidate for its own kernel
once a third machine needs it:

- **Moving-bridge coupling.** A modal string whose end is shaken feels
  `−c_n b̈` with `c_n = 2(−1)^(n+1)/(nπ)`, and pushes on the bridge with
  `Σ (−1)^(n+1) nπT/L q_n`. This is a textbook derivation from the string's
  kinetic energy.
- **Resistive dense-mode board.** Above its first modes a board acts like
  a resistance at the bridge (an infinite plate's mobility `1/(8√(D m''))`,
  Cremer). It is high-passed so the board stays stiff at low frequency.
- **First-order tension modulation.** Each mode's step rotation is bent
  by the head's strain energy. The step stays a rotation, so it stays
  stable, where an explicit stiffness force diverges on the upper modes
  (Berger's approximation; Bilbao 2009 ch. 13).
- **Bounded cubic mode coupling.** One shared coordinate carries a
  quartic potential, so the coupling only moves energy between modes.
  Its stiffness is capped so the explicit step stays stable.
- **Diffuse-field energy store.** The modes too dense to simulate are one
  energy reservoir radiated as band noise: statistical energy analysis
  (Lyon 1975). In the kit it is fed by the strike, not by the ringing
  modes (see below).
- **Lumped collision rattle.** A mass on a spring that collides with a
  body through `contact-solve`. Its collision force, times noise, drives a
  wide resonant bank. This is the snare wires.

## Hammerwork Grand

Per voice (one key), from `piano.fy`:

| part | model |
|---|---|
| strings | Up to 3 unisons: 1 string below B0, 2 below G#2 [G]. The main string carries 56 modes, the partners 16 each. Above the 16th partial the main string's modes stand for every string of the key. Bass keys stride the main string's upper modes (every 2nd to 4th partial, weighted √stride), so a low A still reaches about 7 kHz. |
| partials | `f_n = n f0 √(1+Bn²)/√(1+B)` (Fletcher 1964). B is lowest near C3 (1e-4) and rises about 3 %/semitone into the treble [G, matched to the Kawai: 2.3e-4 at C4]. |
| scale | length `0.05·2^((108−m)·0.0767)` m, capped at 1.95 [G]. Tension 700 N, rising to 1510 N in the bass [G]. Mass per length follows from f0, so string mass is physical. |
| loss | `σ_n = b1 + b3 ω_n²` (Chaigne & Askenfelt 1994), fitted through the fundamental's T60 `30·2^(−(m−21)/26)` s and HF DEC at 4 kHz [G, fitted to the Kawai]. |
| hammer | Mass, stiffness and exponent interpolate Chaigne & Askenfelt's C2/C4/C7 values: 4.9/3.0/2.2 g; K 1e9/4.5e9/1e11; p 2.3/2.5/3.0. Hysteresis α = 0.6·FELT s/m. Speed is 0.25 m/s at velocity 0 to 6 m/s at 127. |
| strike point | 1/8 of the string in the bass to 1/12.5 at the top [G]. The unison strings meet the hammer ±3 % apart, which fills the strike-point notches. |
| bridge / board | 12 plate modes, 58–985 Hz, ζ 1.6–3.4 % [G]. Each key's bridge point lies on a diagonal across the board. A resistive dense-mode term (2e-4·COUPLE s/kg, high-passed at 220 Hz) is what lets the strings lose energy at a realistic rate and gives the 6 dB/oct radiated rise. The shared bridge couples the unisons: in-phase motion drains fast, out-of-phase motion lingers (Weinreich 1977). |
| output | Board acceleration seen from two microphone points. The dense-mode part pans by key (bass left, treble right). |
| dampers | A released key adds decay to each mode by how far the damper point (strike + 3 %) moves in it. RELEASE sets the fundamental's T60. Keys above E7 (MIDI 88) have no damper. PEDAL lifts all dampers live, for held and released notes, re-blended every 32 samples. |

**Measured against the CC0 VCSL Kawai grand** (`v3` layer against
velocity 0.7, partials relative to the fundamental over 0.05–0.35 s,
analysis scripts kept in the session scratch):

| | Kawai C4 | Grand C4 | Kawai C5 | Grand C5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B | 3.0e-4 | 2.3e-4 | 3.1e-4 | 4.8e-4 |
| p2..p6 dB | −2 −19 −13 −9 −26 | 6 −9 −7 −18 −18 | −18 −18 −22 −39 −30 | −7 −20 −24 −29 −29 |
| T60 fundamental | 9.5 s | 5.2 s | 9.7 s | 8.0 s |
| spectral centroid, first 100 ms | 888 Hz | 1238 Hz | 961 Hz | 1384 Hz |

Velocity runs from a 550 Hz centroid at velocity 0.1 to 2.2 kHz at 1.0,
over 26 dB of level. Against the Kawai, the model's C4 is brighter in its
2nd to 4th partials and decays about half as long.

**Cost** (ReleaseFast): about 200 ns per ringing voice, 735 ns for the
bench chord. The render word has more stores than lane mode can stash,
so the 16 voices render scalar ("renders without NEON lanes"). Note-on is
a `call:` composition of seven stages, each under the compiler's
pending-store limit.

**Not modeled:** sympathetic resonance between keys (each voice has its
own board), the duplex scale, longitudinal "phantom" partials, the
second polarization, and key and pedal noise.

## Skinwork Kit

Twelve voices, `note-pitch`, on the GM map. Notes outside 35–59 fold
into it by octave.

| note | piece | body | striker |
|---|---|---|---|
| 35, 36 | kick | membrane, 52 Hz, a 0.229 m head | felt beater, 35 g, p 2.3 |
| 37, 39 | side stick | snare head struck at the rim, light wires | stick across the rim |
| 38, 40 | snare | membrane, 185 Hz, 0.17 m, with wires | hickory stick, 12 g, Hertz p 1.5 |
| 41, 43 | floor tom | membrane, 82 Hz | stick |
| 45, 47 | mid tom | membrane, 118 Hz | stick |
| 48, 50 | high tom | membrane, 165 Hz | stick |
| 42 / 44 / 46 | closed / pedal / open hat | plate, 420 Hz | stick; closed and pedal choke open |
| 49, 52, 55, 57 | crash | plate, 190 Hz | stick |
| 51, 59 | ride | plate, 240 Hz | stick |
| 53 | ride bell | plate, 560 Hz | stick |

The lower note of each tom pair, and the second kick, sit a little lower
(×0.86). Every size, mass, tuning and T60 is an estimate of a typical kit
[G].

- **Heads** use the Bessel ratios, compressed slightly (ratio^0.94) as
  air loading does to a real drum. They are struck at a radius where
  `J_m(j_mn r)` sets each mode's drive. A head's overtones die faster
  than its fundamental, `σ = σ_a (f/f0)^g`: g is 1.6 for the damped kick,
  1.3 for toms, 1 for the snare. (Until 2026-11-11 the loss was
  `s0 + s1 f²` through T60s at f0 and 3 kHz, which left every overtone
  under 1 kHz ringing as long as the fundamental: drums like timpani.)
- **Tension modulation.** The gain is `G = 150/c²` per ply (c is the
  head's wave speed). That follows from the Mylar's elastic stiffness
  against its tension: at 1 mm of excursion a snare head runs 2–3 %
  sharp. GLIDE scales it.
- **Strikers.** A hickory stick is a Hertz contact, `K = 4/3 E* √R`:
  4.4e8 on Mylar, 1.2e9 on bronze. The kick beater is felt. The stick's
  impact force, high-passed twice at about 1 kHz, adds the click. The
  kick's beater slap is a 15 ms burst of noise above 1.5 kHz, about
  30 dB under the body.
- **Snare wires.** A 4 g lumped mass on a 100 Hz spring, resting a hair
  under the head center. WIRES sets the gap. Collisions drive eight wide
  buzz modes from 2.1 kHz up, and the strands go on shaking after a
  collision (T60 0.2 s × the snare's DECAY), which is the snare's tail:
  the level of the last collision, decaying, drives the buzz modes with
  noise and adds plain noise between them.
- **Fitted to recordings (2026-11-11).** The kit is tuned against the
  VCSL library's real suspended cymbal, hi-hat, snare (wires on and off)
  and toms, by comparing band levels over time (five bands, six windows
  to 3.5 s, relative to the peak). What that showed, against the
  guesses before it: a cymbal's spectrum is flat per Hz from 150 Hz to
  20 kHz at the hit; its lows ring for many seconds while the top falls
  20 dB in 300 ms (`σ = s0 + s1 f²`, T60 8–12 s at the lowest mode,
  1.4–1.8 s at 8 kHz); a hi-hat decays at nearly one rate across the
  band (0.6 s closed, 3 s open); a snare's wires hold the 1–8 kHz band
  for about 100 ms, 18 dB under the body; a tom's overtone band is as
  loud as its fundamental at the hit.
- **Stick on a head.** The 40 modes kept are too few to carry the local
  give of a membrane under a stick, so with the Hertz stiffness the
  stick left in two samples and chattered: a full-height click and a
  body ten times quieter. That compliance is now in the contact
  (K = 10^6.3–6.5 for toms and snare), which lasts about a millisecond.
- **Cymbals (since 2026-11-11).** Three plates, hats, crash and ride,
  live in the machine's shared region and persist between hits: a second
  hit adds to a plate that is still ringing, and closing the hats is
  damping written onto the same plate, which is the choke. Each is 160
  modes at constant density from its lowest mode (380, 170, 230 Hz) to
  13.5–15 kHz, each mode placed at random in its own cell, as exact
  rotations with loss `σ = s0 + s1 f²` through T60s at the lowest
  mode and at 8 kHz. A hit is a raised-cosine force pulse, two samples
  at full velocity and up to 0.1 ms longer when soft (STICK scales it),
  so the attack is flat to 20 kHz. Modes run to 19 kHz; those above
  8 kHz stay off the springs, because near Nyquist the explicit kick
  resonates with the step and gains energy. The
  nonlinearity is four springs per plate: mode i hangs on spring
  i mod 4, a spring's coordinate is `u = Σ a_i q_i` and its force
  `g(u) = G u0 x²(β + x)/(1 + x²)`, `x = u/u0`. It is quadratic then
  cubic, as a shallow shell's is, has a potential (so it only moves
  energy between modes) and a bounded slope (so the explicit kick is
  stable; G = 30 at 48 kHz, 60 is not). The products of the low modes
  drive the high ones: a hard crash's top octave rises about 6 dB over
  the first 20–100 ms, a soft one's less. BLOOM scales G, and past 1
  lowers u0. This reduced coupling stands in for the von Kármán plate,
  which needs some 800 modes at 100 kHz (Ducceschi & Touzé 2015; Nguyen
  & Touzé 2019); a single shared cubic term, as before, is a Berger-type
  model, which glides but does not cascade (Bilbao et al. 2023). There
  is no noise source in the cymbals. The plates were prototyped in numpy
  first. Each plate has its own gated shared word (docs/04), so it costs
  nothing until hit; a ringing plate is about 1.5 µs per sample.
- **Plates, the old per-voice model (unused for cymbals now).** 40 modes on a stretched, jittered series
  `((i + 1.2)/1.2)^1.17`. Strike weights go as ratio^t: t is +0.7 for
  hats, −0.35 crash, −0.2 ride, −0.7 bell. A bounded cubic coupling
  (BLOOM) moves energy between them. The diffuse field is band noise,
  1.8–4 kHz high-pass and 8.5 kHz two-pole low-pass, kept well under the
  modes (a first pass had it louder and longer, which read as hiss). The stick's kinetic
  energy passes into it over the piece's bloom time (2 ms closed hat,
  4 ms open, 20 ms crash, 30 ms ride) and it decays at the piece's
  high-frequency T60, so a cymbal is brightest just after the hit.
  (Before, the field was fed continuously by the modes' strain energy:
  it swelled over a second with no attack and lasted as long as the
  lowest partial.) The diffuse field and the kick's noise slap are
  switched off. The wires' tail is back, set from the recorded snare
  and rolled off at 4.5 kHz.

**Cost:** about 480 ns per sample in Debug for the bench hit sequence.
The render word, like the piano's, renders without NEON lanes.

## Fretwire: pickups, wiring and bodies

**Status:** built 2026-10-16 on branch `claude/fretwire-bodies`, stacked
on `claude/physical-piano-drums` (it needs shared state, docs/04).

Fretwire's strings were already waveguides fitted to recordings. What
they lacked was the instrument: one pickup point and a resonant lowpass
stood for every electric, and nothing joined the strings. Each machine
now has a second page (GUITAR or BASS) with three sections, and every
string drives one shared body through the bridge. The shared word
(`fw-body-render`, period 1) runs after the strings each sample.

### Pickups

Two pickups, NECK and BRIDGE, each at its own point along the string
(a fraction of the open string, from the bridge). A coil hears the string
where it sits, so its comb notches move string to string and up the neck,
as on the instrument. SELECT picks NECK, BOTH, BRIDGE, or OUT (both, the
bridge coil reversed). Two coils in parallel share the output by
inductance, each heard in the proportion of the other's.

| TYPE | Taps | Inductance | Sounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| SINGLE | one point | 1x | the bright, glassy single coil |
| P-90 (guitar) | a wide bobbin's edges, 0.036 of the string apart | 1.8x | fatter, rawer mids |
| HUMBUCK | two coils 18 mm apart | 1.8x | warmer: the two coils' views cancel near an open string's 36th harmonic (3 dB down at the 18th), and the resonance drops to 0.75x |
| SPLIT (bass) | one point, the low strings' half 1.2% further toward the neck | 1.4x | a P-type bass |

HEIGHT moves the magnets toward the strings. The coil's output is the
string's velocity times 1 / [1 - k x]^2, x the string's displacement over
it [Horton and Moore 2009]. A string swinging toward the magnet sees more
flux than swinging away, so loud notes gain even harmonics and some
level.

### Wiring

The coils drive the cable's capacitance, the pots and the amp's input:

    H(s) = 1 / (1 + (R + sL) (sC + G + sCt / (1 + s Rt Ct)))

- **RESO** and **PEAK** (the old TONE and RES, same ids) are a single
  coil's loaded resonance with the tone pot open. C and G are solved from
  them for the selected coils' L and R, so a humbucker or two coils in
  parallel move the resonance by the square root of their inductance.
- **TONE** is the tone pot: down from 1 its resistance (250 k, audio
  taper) brings in the tone capacitor (22 nF guitar, 47 nF bass). At 0
  the resonance drops to around 700 Hz: the "woman tone". At 1 the
  branch is open, the filter is the old 2-pole one, and the output nulls
  against the old Fretwire's at the 24-bit floor (COUPLE 0).
- **VOL** is the volume pot: an audio taper, plus the treble its wiper
  resistance loses into the cable. Turning it down cleans up Ampstack's
  drive the way a guitarist does.

The 3rd-order H goes to z by the bilinear transform, prewarped at the
resonance, once a block.

### Body

Every string pushes on the bridge with the wave arriving there and moves
with the bridge's velocity [Gough 1981; Woodhouse 2004]. The bridge is
the body's modes: 8 low modes per body (the air cavity, the first top and
back modes), then 40 dense modes spread evenly to a few kHz with
overlapping bandwidths. A mode takes energy from string partials near it,
so notes there decay faster and swell into the body: dead spots on a
solid body, the wood of a hollow one. The 12-mode board taught us the
dense part matters: a dozen peaks alone sound boxy and honky.

| BODY | Coupling | Character |
|---|---|---|
| SOLID | weak, high Q | neck and body dead spots; almost no air |
| SEMI (guitar) | a centre block | some wood, sustain kept |
| HOLLOW | an archtop, or a violin bass | woody, shorter, an air resonance near 110 Hz (bass 88) |
| ACOUSTIC | a flat-top, or an acoustic bass guitar | strong: 98 Hz air, 196 Hz top (bass 72 / 128) |

The low modes follow published guitar mobility and radiation
measurements in shape, not any one instrument (our own estimates).

- **COUPLE** scales the bridge coupling. 0 is a rigid bridge, the old
  Fretwire.
- **AIR** mixes in the body's radiated sound: its modes' acceleration
  and, above them, the bridge force as a resistance. At 1 on an acoustic
  body it sits at the pickups' level. A solid body radiates about 40 dB
  less, as an unplugged electric does.
- **PIEZO** mixes an under-saddle piezo: the bridge force itself, bright
  and direct.
- **SYMP** rings the open strings in sympathy. Each open string is a
  delay loop in the shared region, driven by the bridge and pushing back
  on it. A string a note is played on is muted. The strings are heard
  through the pickups and the body.

### Frets: buzz, slap and pop

BUZZ puts the frets in reach. The string's displacement over the last
fret (0.3 of the open string from the bridge) is the running sum of its
velocity there: this sample's loop write less the wave that passed that
point one comb delay earlier. It cannot pass the gap. What would carry it
past is taken out of the wave leaving the fret one way and put into the
wave leaving the other, the same amount in both, so a knock can reflect
or lose energy and never add it. BUZZ 0 is off (and bit-exact with the
kernel before); raising it closes the gap from 4.5% to 1% of a period of
summed velocity, where a hard finger pluck swings about 3%.

- **SLAP** is a thumb. The pulse is up to twice a finger's speed and
  stops when it has carried the string to the fret (0.5 to 1.1 gaps, by
  velocity), over about 70 µs. That stop is the click; velocity sets how
  fast the string gets there, so how bright.
- **A pop** is a pluck (SHAPE 1) from the far side, pulled 0.4 to 9.4
  gaps by velocity squared, which meets the frets half a period after it
  is let go and rattles until it has lost enough.
- With SLAP up, a note on the upper half of the strings is popped and
  the rest are thumbed. HAND decides the string, so it decides the split:
  the `slap` preset (HAND 1.5) thumbs up to D#2 and pops from E2.

Measured against solo slap recordings (three, CC0 and CC-BY, lossy, with
their own EQ): a real slap's content above 2 kHz arrives in a burst 4 to
6 ms wide that returns once a period and falls about 10 dB over three
periods, and 3 to 8 kHz falls about 18 dB in the first 150 ms. The limiter
this replaces had its highs 15 dB lower and gone in 200 ms. The model now
has the bursts and the fall (thumbed E1, 3 to 8 kHz: −26, −30, −39 dB re
peak at 6 to 20, 20 to 60 and 60 to 200 ms; the recording −25, −34, −43).
Not matched: the recordings' scooped mids (theirs is 10 dB lower at 150
to 800 Hz, likely EQ), and nothing here has been fitted to a clean DI
slap note, because none was to hand.

### Fitted against recordings

`tools/fit/string_fit.py` measures the attack spectrum (harmonics 1-16,
20-80 ms) and each harmonic's T60. A coordinate-descent fit over the
pickup, pluck, wiring and decay knobs, scored on both, set two presets:

| Preset | Reference | Error per harmonic, start → fit |
|---|---|---|
| `guitar` twin-humbucker | Karoryfer Emily, C3, both humbuckers, direct | 11.0 → 7.5 dB |
| `bass` jazz-both | Karoryfer Growlybass, C2 (their c3), both pickups, direct | 12.8 → 7.8 dB |

What still differs: the guitar reference's spectrum falls smoothly where
ours keeps the pluck's and pickups' comb notches. The bass reference
shows them (its 5th harmonic is 30 dB down), so they are real. But a pick
and two pickups fill them in more than the model does.

The fingered roundwound decay (`default`, `p-bass`, `fingers-bridge`,
`pick`, and the knobs' defaults) is fitted to Karoryfer Fashionbass at
F1, D2 and D3: DECAY 19 s, HF DEC 0.27 s, KNEE 24. With the knee that
high the shelf's loss rises as the harmonic number squared across the
whole audible range, which is how the recording decays. T60 in seconds,
model / recording, at D2: harmonic 1 16 / 19, 4 6.0 / 6.6, 8 2.0 / 3.9,
12 1.3 / 1.3, 16 0.8 / 0.8, 20 0.6 / 0.5. The old values (12, 1.2, 9)
had harmonics 3 to 8 ringing a third to half as long as the recording
and those above 12 twice as long. Not matched: the recording's
harmonic-to-harmonic scatter (its 2nd rings 5 s between a 19 s 1st and a
21 s 3rd), and its attack has no notch as deep as the model's pluck comb.

### Cost

The shared body makes the strings render in lockstep, one voice at a
time. In ReleaseFast the guitar's chord case went from 176 to 480 ns per
sample (2.3% of a core). SYMP adds about 100 ns while it is up.

## Rhodes E-Piano: the pickup, fitted

The voice (`kernels/06-voices/rhodes.fy`) is a sine tine in front of a
pickup whose flux is 1 / (1 + (x − off)²); the output is its rate of
change. That law was checked against a sampled 1977 Mark I Stage 73
(jRhodes3d, direct from the harp, five velocity layers, 15 keys): for each
key and layer, the swing and offset that best explain harmonics 1 to 8.
It fits to 1 to 2.5 dB almost everywhere, with one offset per key and a
swing that rises layer by layer, so the law stays and the numbers change:

- **Swing.** At full velocity the tine swings 2.3 pickup widths at D3,
  4 to 5 in the bottom octave and 0.4 at E5: (D3 / f)^1.15, held at
  twice D3's. The old voice swung 0.7 at every key, so it never barked:
  a hard real note has its 2nd harmonic 16 dB over the fundamental and a
  series to the 9th. DRIVE is the swing at D3 (0.6 to 4.2, 0.5 is the
  measured piano); the output is divided by the key's share, and the
  keys stay level within 1.5 dB.
- **Touch.** The softest layer swings 0.13 to 0.2 of the hardest, which
  velocity^1.4 already gives. The recording shows no extra attack
  brightness beyond the swing, so BARK is near 0 in the presets.
- **Offset.** 0.35 to 1.0 from key to key. VOICING sets the middle and
  each key gets its own within ±0.125.
- **Decay.** T60 of the fundamental is 35 to 40 s in the bottom octave,
  24 s at D3, 14 s at B4, 9 s at D6: (C3 / f)^0.5, and faster again above
  1.7 kHz (1.7 s at C7). DECAY 0.5 is the measured piano.
- **The tine's second mode** is at 7.35 times the fundamental, 38 dB
  under it. The old ping was at 6.27 and 30 dB louder, the loudest thing
  in a soft note. BELL 0.36 is the measured level; BELL 1 is 12 dB under
  the fundamental, for effect.
- CHORUS (two fundamentals beating) is 0 in the presets: a tine has one.

Not modeled: a low thud in the first 40 ms of the recording (a 60 to
100 Hz mode, heard as sidebands 30 dB down), the hammer tip's change of
hardness across the keyboard, the damper's felt, and the author's EQ
(a treble boost and low-mid scoop, so absolute tilt was not fitted).
`songs/rhodes_demo.py` plays one progression at three velocities.

## Presets

- `grand`: default, bright-pop, felt-ballad, honky-tonk, sustain-wash,
  dry-studio.
- `kit`: default, jazz, rock, funk-dry, gong-wash.
- `guitar` (new or reworked here): twin-humbucker, p90-crunch,
  semi-hollow, out-of-phase, acoustic; jazz is now a hollow archtop on
  the neck humbucker, funk the middle and bridge single coils together.
- `bass`: jazz-both, p-bass, hollow, acoustic; `slap` (thumb and pop
  from one track), `thumb` and `pop` are on both single coils with the
  pickup's peak up.

## Next

- **Fretwire and Rhodes.** Fretwire's frets are one point; a slap could
  use the whole fingerboard, and the fingered decay is still a first-order
  shelf where the measured bass's low partials ring 2 to 3 times longer.
  Rhodes has no hammer or tonebar yet: a struck tine-and-tonebar pair
  would give the attack thud the recording has.
- **Lane mode.** Splitting the render into a lane-eligible word, or
  raising the stash limit, would roughly halve the cost.
- **Sympathetic strings in the Grand.** Fretwire's open strings show the
  way: a shared region holding the undamped strings, driven by the
  bridge.
